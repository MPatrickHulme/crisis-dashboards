Congress and the Use of Force — Twenty-Nine Crises, 1898–2026

Dynamic congressional sentiment and roll call votes
Reading the figures. Left axis: Congressional Sentiment Score — the mean per-lawmaker position on U.S. military force (−0.5 oppose … +0.5 support) from a 3-model ensemble (Grok + Luna + Gemini) classifying every congressional floor statement, press release, tweet and Facebook post in each crisis window; relevant statements only; periods with enough scored statements (weekly for most crises, monthly for the multi-year Vietnam, Bosnia and Spanish–American windows; thresholds 3–8 by corpus volume); Periods carrying fewer than that many speakers are not estimated; the line is interpolated across them so the trend reads continuously, and the shaded band shows the distance from zero — above the dashed line Congress was on balance for the use of force, below it against; solid = tripartite instrument, dashed = 8-item instrument. Right axis: share voting to support U.S. force on that crisis’s use-of-force roll calls — for authorization measures the share voting Yea, for withdrawal / funds-cutoff measures the share voting Nay (each tooltip states what Yea meant), so authorizations and restrictions plot on one support-up scale, and CSS 0 aligns with the 50% line for direct comparison of words and votes. Large purple markers = whole chamber (diamond = Senate, square = House); small red/blue diamonds on the same stem = Republican and Democratic shares; a gold halo marks measures that carried. Hover any marker for the bill, tally and party split with links to the sources (voteview covers every Congress; congress.gov where coverage exists); click a marker to open the primary source. Gold dotted vertical line: the crisis’s U.S. use-of-force date (absent where the U.S. never struck). Bottom panels: weekly statement volume by party (blue D, red R, gray other/unknown). Every roll call was independently verified against official sources (Senate/House Clerk pages, voteview, GovTrack, congress.gov).
